About

Building reliable learning systems from human signals to robot action

I combine robot learning, computer vision, multimodal sensing, and safety-aware decision making to build systems that move from raw data to real-robot evaluation.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Aerospace & Mechanical Engineering at Saint Louis University, advised by Prof. Madi Babaiasl in the MechaRithm Lab. I work end to end: designing experiments, synchronizing multimodal data, building perception and learning pipelines, evaluating failure modes, and integrating models with ROS 2 for real-robot studies.

  • Human intent → robot actionEEG, EMG, and gaze fusion for intent recognition under sensor degradation and dropout.
  • Human video → robot skillsRGB-D perception, hand/object understanding, trajectory retargeting, and diffusion-policy learning from ordinary demonstrations.
  • Confidence → safer decisionsCalibration, reliability estimation, feasibility checks, and policy-level evaluation so uncertainty changes what the robot does.

Earlier research and interdisciplinary background

Before robotics I spent two years as a research assistant at Fordham University working on multimodal NLP and clinical language models. I first-authored SumGPT, a T5 + Vision Transformer framework for radiology report summarization that reached a ROUGE-1 of 0.8514 and a BLEU of 0.8470 against VisualGPT, T5 + CLIP and XLM-RoBERTa + ViT baselines; and MemesViTa, a ViT + DeBERTa fusion model for troll-meme detection at 94.29% accuracy and 95.82% F1 — 41.3% above the strongest vision-only baseline, and well clear of GPT-4 in a 50-shot setting. I also co-authored MediGPT, which benchmarked ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 against fine-tuned and prompt-tuned language models for medical text classification, improving on them by 13.6–22.3% across four datasets. That work is where I learned to treat ablation, error analysis and calibration as first-class concerns — the same discipline I now apply to robots.

I completed my M.Sc. in Data Science at Fordham University on a GSAS Centennial Scholarship, and interned as a data analyst at Authentic Brands Group in New York, automating ETL and reporting over Salesforce and SQL sources. Earlier, at Kyungsung University in South Korea, I researched switched reluctance machines for flywheel energy storage and finished first in my class in Mechatronics Engineering. Three degrees across three countries left one through-line: hardware, signals and language are all the same problem once you ask a system to say how much it trusts its own input.

Research focus

Reliable perception and learning for robots that operate under uncertainty

My research is organized around one deployment question: what evidence should a robot trust, and how should uncertainty change its action? I study that question through multimodal sensing, human-video learning, and decision-level safety.

Multimodal fusion

Transformer and state-space fusion across EEG, EMG, gaze and RGB-D — with cross-modal attention that is gated on measured reliability rather than on content, so a dead channel is down-weighted instead of hallucinated through.

Question

How should a model fuse modalities when their quality changes over time?

Approach

Reliability-gated multimodal transformers/state-space models across EEG, EMG, gaze, and vision.

Robot learning from human video

Diffusion policies trained on RGB-D recordings of people, not teleoperation. Hand-pose estimation, SAM2 segmentation, ICP refinement and Cartesian retargeting turn ordinary footage into executable Kinova Gen3 trajectories.

Question

Can robot skills be learned from ordinary human demonstrations instead of robot teleoperation?

Approach

RGB-D hand/object perception, geometric retargeting, embodiment matching, and diffusion policies.

Decision-centric safety

Commit-readiness scores, dwell/hysteresis filtering, geometric feasibility checks and calibrated confidence — evaluated with policy-level metrics (false commits per 1k rest windows, missed intent) rather than accuracy alone.

Question

How should confidence affect a robot decision—not just a classification score?

Approach

Commit-readiness, calibration, hysteresis, feasibility checks, and policy-level safety metrics.

NeuroCommitSSM

Knowing when it is safe to move — not just what the user wants

A wheelchair-mounted arm that misreads intent is irritating. One that starts moving while its user is sitting still is dangerous — and that is the failure an accuracy score never shows. I built a model that rates, moment by moment, how ready the user actually is to commit, paired with a supervisor that will not release motion unless the camera can see the target and the arm can genuinely reach it.

On 32 subjects it produces 0.75 false starts per 1,000 rest windows, against 6.5–9.4 for transformer, TCN and GRU baselines. Take the muscle sensor away and those baselines collapse past 80 false starts; this one drops to 0.12, because losing a modality changes what the model trusts instead of being ignored.

TriSaFe-Trans

Fusing brain, muscle and gaze so one failing sensor cannot take the system down

EEG, EMG and eye tracking each catch a different part of the same intention, and each breaks in its own way — electrodes shift, gaze drifts out of calibration, brain signals change between sessions. A fusion model that assumes all three are healthy keeps trusting a channel that has gone dead. I built a transformer that weights each stream by how reliable it currently is, pretrained on unlabelled recordings before fine-tuning.

It beats three tri-modal baselines across 20 subjects with statistical significance, and the ablations show exactly why fusing is worth it: muscle activity tells you when someone is acting, gaze tells you which task, and neither is sufficient alone. I also built the dataset it runs on.

Video-to-Robot Imitation Learning

Learning manipulation from ordinary human video, with a gate for when vision fails

Teaching a robot a new household task normally means physically guiding the arm hundreds of times. I film a person instead and turn that footage into robot training data: locate the hand in 3D, segment it from the scene, erase the person from the video, and composite a simulated Kinova back in from the same calibrated viewpoint. A diffusion policy learns from what comes out.

The research contribution is what happens when the camera has a bad moment. A live estimate of perception quality pauses the robot on the frames it cannot support, cutting action error 11%. Pausing at random, or on a fixed schedule, gives no benefit whatsoever — which is what proves the gain comes from picking the right moments rather than from pausing at all.

Remaining before a planned IEEE T-RO submission are the final ablations and an embodiment-matching step that keeps deployment inputs aligned with the policy’s training distribution.

Reliability-Aware Fusion

Rebuilding a study so that its result can actually be believed

Rebuilding the preprocessing for this dataset turned up five defects that invalidated every earlier result — roughly a third of the brain-signal samples duplicated, a declared sensor latency never applied, and held-out subjects leaking into training. I fixed the pipeline, then wrote down the claims, the eleven baselines and the stopping rules before running anything, including the admission that 22 subjects cannot support a small accuracy win however the numbers come out.

Each of the model's three mechanisms is built so its own ablation can kill it. That converts the contribution from an accuracy claim the data cannot carry into one it can: fusion architecture barely moves accuracy at this sample size, but reliability-aware gating changes how the system fails.

Current work & next validation step

I am training the eleven baseline arms across 22 leave-one-subject-out folds. The proposed model remains untrained until those results land, so the baseline evidence determines whether the architecture claim is worth making. The next tests focus on modality-dropout robustness and calibration, with Information Fusion as the target venue.

MemesViTa

Where a meme turns hostile is in the gap between image and caption

Troll memes defeat single-modality detectors because the picture and the text are each innocuous alone. Fusing a Vision Transformer with DeBERTa closes that gap: 94.29% accuracy against 4,368 training and 1,092 test pairs — 41.3% above the best vision-only model, and far ahead of GPT-4 prompted with 50 examples, which reached 37.00% F1.
